\begin{abstract}
AVAC4QGIS is a QGIS framework for spatially explicit simulation of snow
avalanche propagation and, optionally, the resulting impulse-wave forcing.
AVAC advances a Cartesian, depth-averaged granular-flow model on a fixed DEM
with Coulomb--Voellmy resistance, a curvature-dependent normal-load correction,
finite terrain-tangent velocity transport, an analytical frozen-coefficient
basal-source update, and static-yield arrest. Conservative shallow-layer
correction-flux damping and synchronized CFL preflight complement the
wet--dry finite-volume scheme. Voellmy source updates retain an exact zero
even on a super-yield bed; the legacy pure-Coulomb fallback is kept separate.
WAVE subsequently advances the standard GeoClaw
shallow-water equations on a separately selected terrain/bathymetry surface.
The offline coupling transfers positive avalanche volume flux and horizontal
momentum across a polygon-derived shoreline. This paper specifies the
implemented equations, numerical treatment, geospatial preprocessing, and
physical scope of the two solvers, including the assumptions and current
implementation limitations that affect interpretation of their outputs.
\end{abstract}

\section{AVAC4QGIS model system}
\label{sec:model-system}

AVAC4QGIS organizes two depth-averaged solvers in a single geospatial
workflow. AVAC represents avalanche release and propagation as a mobile
depth layer and can be used independently. It has no erosion, entrainment,
compaction, mass source, deposited-bed evolution, or changing topography;
its final-time depth is therefore an arrested-layer field rather than a
separate physical deposition model. When the optional impulse-wave workflow is
selected, a completed AVAC calculation supplies a time-dependent source to
WAVE, which represents wave generation, propagation, and inundation. The
coupling is sequential and one-way: WAVE reads AVAC output, whereas the water
does not alter the preceding avalanche calculation.

Both solvers are developments of GeoClaw, an open-source finite-volume code
for depth-averaged geophysical flows with wetting and drying
\citep{George2008,BergerEtAl2011}. This common numerical basis gives the two
states the same conservative structure, but it does not make the two physical
models interchangeable. AVAC adds a basal snow-avalanche rheology and a
static-yield treatment; WAVE retains the standard water equations and Manning
friction. Their shared state representation is instead used to define an
explicit shoreline transfer operator.

The description below follows the same order as a simulation. We first define
the geospatial inputs and the two available workflows, then state the AVAC and
WAVE equations exactly as implemented, and finally define the transfer between
them. This ordering distinguishes the governing equations from the QGIS and
runtime infrastructure that makes them accessible.

\subsection{System overview and workflow}
\label{sec:system-overview}

In the standalone workflow, the user supplies a digital elevation model (DEM)
and one or more avalanche-release polygons. The polygons are transformed to
the DEM coordinate reference system; because AVAC solves Cartesian equations,
that system must express horizontal distances in consistent metric units.
AVAC4QGIS rasterizes the polygons against the DEM cells, creates the initial mobile-snow
layer, writes an isolated run directory, and executes AVAC. The principal
results are time-dependent flow depth and momentum, maximum depth and speed,
and the final-time mobile-depth field. The latter is an arrested deposit only
when the calculation has reached rest by its selected final time. No lake data
or WAVE execution is required for this branch.

The impulse-wave workflow adds a terrain/bathymetry DEM, a polygon delimiting
the water body, an absolute initial water-surface elevation, and WAVE
hydraulic parameters. It requires a successfully completed AVAC run. The WAVE
rectangular domain is the AVAC domain, while the polygon determines the
initial lake and the shoreline across which avalanche flux is sampled. The
WAVE terrain/bathymetry DEM must cover that rectangle and share its coordinate
reference system, but it is a separately selected surface and need not equal
the fixed AVAC bed. Consequently, the source and receiving calculations share
a coordinate system and spatial coverage even when their terrain resolutions
and solver cell sizes differ.

AVAC output frames remain immutable during preparation of the WAVE scenario.
The plugin reconstructs the lake on the WAVE grid, samples the saved AVAC
states along its shoreline, writes a conservative source time series, and then
starts WAVE. This separation makes the avalanche result reusable: several lake
levels, coupling factors, or hydraulic parameter sets can be evaluated without
rerunning AVAC.

The two branches return to a common results workspace in QGIS
\citep{QGIS2026}. Run metadata retain the materialized input layers,
scientific parameters, solver configuration, source-run identifier, and
runtime identity. Temporal raster layers, profiles, gauges, summary maps, and
exports are therefore linked to the calculation that produced them rather
than being handled as independent files.

\subsection{AVAC governing equations and numerical method}
\label{sec:avac-method}

\subsubsection{Conserved variables, coordinates, and terrain representation}

AVAC is solved on a Cartesian map grid $(x,y)$ over a fixed bed elevation
$B(x,y)$. Its conservative state is
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{q}_a=(h_a,m_{x,a},m_{y,a})^{\mathsf T}
  =(h_a,h_a u_a,h_a v_a)^{\mathsf T},
  \label{eq:avac-state}
\end{equation}
where $h_a$ is vertical flow depth and $(u_a,v_a)$ are the two horizontal,
depth-averaged velocity components. This convention is important because
many avalanche models instead use depth normal to the terrain. Whenever a
terrain-tangent speed is reported, AVAC4QGIS reconstructs the vertical
component $w_a=u_a B_x+v_a B_y$ and evaluates
$U_{a,s}=\sqrt{u_a^2+v_a^2+w_a^2}$. This is a geometric reconstruction used
by the basal-law projections and diagnostics. The conserved variables remain a
vertical depth and horizontal map-plane momenta: AVAC neither stores a
terrain-tangent momentum state nor an independent local frame. It does apply
a discrete transport between estimated departure and arrival tangent planes,
as described below.

The initial momenta are zero. Within the release polygons, the initial depth
can be uniform, $h_a=d_0$, or corrected for elevation and slope. The optional
elevation increment is
\begin{equation}
  d_z=(B-z_{\mathrm{ref}})G_z/100,
  \label{eq:release-elevation}
\end{equation}
where $G_z$ is the snow-depth change per \SI{100}{m}. The optional slope
factor is
\begin{equation}
  f_s=\frac{\sin\theta_c-\nu\cos\theta_c}
           {\sin\theta-\nu\cos\theta},
  \qquad \theta=\tan^{-1}\!\left(\lVert\nabla B\rVert\right),
  \label{eq:release-slope}
\end{equation}
and is applied only where $\theta>25^\circ$ and the absolute value of the
denominator exceeds $10^{-12}$;
else it is set to zero. Thus the four supported choices inside the release
mask are $d_0$, $d_0+d_z$, $d_0f_s$, and $(d_0+d_z)f_s$. Outside that mask the
mobile depth is zero. The cell-centred release raster is conservatively
constructed in two stages. QGIS reports the selected raster extent as outer
pixel edges. AVAC4QGIS therefore represents sample $i,j$ by the common DEM and
release-cell centres
\begin{equation}
  x_i=x_{\min}+(i-\tfrac12)\Delta x,\qquad
  y_j=y_{\min}+(j-\tfrac12)\Delta y,
  \label{eq:avac-qgis-cell-centres}
\end{equation}
for $i=1,\ldots,N_x$ and $j=1,\ldots,N_y$, while retaining
$(x_{\min},x_{\max},y_{\min},y_{\max})$ as physical outer edges in terrain
metadata. First, the fraction of each DEM cell occupied by
the union of the release polygons is estimated on a regular $64\times64$
subcell grid. Exterior rings, holes, multipart features, and overlapping
polygons are handled jointly, so the fraction remains between zero and one;
this stage is a high-resolution area approximation rather than an exact
polygon--cell intersection. Second, the resulting piecewise-constant raster
is conservatively averaged onto each solver cell from the exact rectangular
overlap with the source cells. If the solver spacing is $r\Delta x$, $r$ must
be an integer and both $N_x$ and $N_y$ must be divisible by $r$. A
non-divisible selected DEM is rejected: silently cropping it would lose QGIS
release cells, whereas extending its domain would add extrapolated terrain and
qinit-free solver cells.

GeoClaw's topotype-3 reader shifts an ESRI \texttt{xllcorner} to the first
terrain-sample centre and interpolates the supplied elevations as nodal data.
AVAC4QGIS consequently writes a one-sample, replicated terrain halo only for
that topography file. For a QGIS DEM extending from $x_{\min}$ to
$x_{\max}$, the topography samples span from
$x_{\min}-\Delta x/2$ to $x_{\max}+\Delta x/2$ (and analogously in $y$),
which brackets every retained solver edge. The release coverage and binary
initializer are not padded: the latter writes the original western and
northern cell centres and native spacings, from which its reader reconstructs
the original source-cell edges by adding and subtracting one half-cell. Thus
the QGIS terrain cells, release cells, qinit cells, and solver rectangle are
co-registered; the replicated halo supplies interpolation support rather than
additional terrain or initialized mass. GeoClaw's maximum-value fixed grid
(FGmax) instead specifies its first and last output \emph{sample points}.
AVAC4QGIS supplies those points at the finite-volume cell centres in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:avac-qgis-cell-centres}, rather than at the physical outer
edges. It consequently writes $N_x\times N_y$ peak samples; when the QGIS
reader restores each sample's half-cell envelope, the maximum products cover
their configured output rectangle without a half-cell shift or an extra row or
column. The time-dependent fixed-grid products use their own edge-based
GeoClaw convention and have the same raster registration when they share that
domain. Raw FGmax files produced with the former edge-sample setup must be
regenerated by rerunning: their samples do not contain the missing cell-centre
values, so a GIS georeferencing change alone cannot repair them.
A local fine DEM is instead an overlay
on that base field. It cannot receive the external replicated halo, because
GeoClaw gives the finer topography file priority wherever its nodal envelope
overlaps the base terrain. AVAC4QGIS therefore writes the fine file on a
half-spacing node grid whose first and last nodes lie exactly on the fine
QGIS outer edges. Its edge and midpoint values sample the same replicated-edge
bilinear surface within the fine footprint, while the base DEM remains active
at every exterior point. Thus the local terrain is neither extrapolated over
an undocumented half-cell fringe nor truncated at its own boundary. Prepared
\texttt{init.xyz} and
\texttt{init.avacbin} files
written by earlier plugin versions must be regenerated: their format already
defined the listed coordinates as cell centres, but the former writer violated
that contract. AVAC4QGIS validates the release controls before evaluating the
corrections and requires the unscaled candidate depth in every covered cell to
be finite and non-negative. A release polygon that covers a non-finite DEM
cell, a non-finite control, or a slope/elevation correction that produces a
negative candidate is rejected with its affected cell location; it is not
silently converted to a dry cell. The fractional coverage factor is applied
only after this check, and both qinit writers independently reject non-finite
or negative stored values. Thus dry-state handling cannot alter an invalid
initial mass field after preparation.

Terrain elevations and ASCII terrain coordinates are serialized with
binary64 round-trip precision (17 significant decimal digits for elevations).
This avoids introducing artificial second differences by rounding a planar
DEM during preparation; it does not recover precision absent from the source
DEM. It also does not eliminate roundoff or conditioning errors in GeoClaw's
subsequent topographic cell integration, especially on fine AMR patches.

\subsubsection{Mass and momentum equations}

The active AVAC solver advances the Cartesian shallow-water balance laws
\begin{align}
  \partial_t h_a
    +\partial_x(h_a u_a)+\partial_y(h_a v_a) &=0,
    \label{eq:avac-mass}\\
  \partial_t(h_a u_a)
    +\partial_x\!\left(h_a u_a^2+\tfrac12 g h_a^2\right)
    +\partial_y(h_a u_a v_a)
    &=-g h_a\,\partial_x B+S_{x,r}+S_{x,g},
    \label{eq:avac-xmomentum}\\
  \partial_t(h_a v_a)
    +\partial_x(h_a u_a v_a)
    +\partial_y\!\left(h_a v_a^2+\tfrac12 g h_a^2\right)
    &=-g h_a\,\partial_y B+S_{y,r}+S_{y,g}.
    \label{eq:avac-ymomentum}
\end{align}
Here $g$ is gravitational acceleration,
$\boldsymbol{S}_r=(S_{x,r},S_{y,r})$ combines basal resistance with the
flow-parallel steep-slope correction, and
$\boldsymbol{S}_g=(S_{x,g},S_{y,g})$ denotes the differential limit of the
separate terrain-tangent transport operator below. The
conservative fluxes, topographic source, and wet--dry Riemann problem are those
of GeoClaw \citep{George2008,BergerEtAl2011}; the rheology and arrest logic
described below are AVAC-specific.

Equations~\eqref{eq:avac-mass}--\eqref{eq:avac-ymomentum} are map-plane
shallow-water equations rather than surface-fitted Savage--Hutter equations.
Their pressure flux is hydrostatic and isotropic, corresponding to an
earth-pressure coefficient of one; no active--passive earth-pressure closure
is introduced. Relief enters the conservative balance through $\nabla B$.
The directional Hessian projection $\kappa_v$ affects the moving basal
normal-load correction below, but does not enter the hydrostatic pressure flux
and is distinct from terrain-tangent velocity transport. Standard Cartesian
shallow-water gravity is too large on a steep surface because the surface
gradient is a tangent rather than a sine and because only the horizontal
acceleration is evolved. AVAC accounts for both projections in its
flow-parallel source term following the bed-gradient variant of
\citet{HergartenRobl2015}. This distinction is retained when AVAC is compared
with models that express thickness and momentum in a terrain-following frame.

\subsubsection{Basal rheology}

AVAC offers Coulomb, Voellmy, and cohesive Voellmy resistance. The first two
follow the two-parameter avalanche-resistance family introduced by
\citet{Voellmy1955} and subsequently used in operational avalanche calculation
\citep{SalmEtAl1990,ChristenEtAl2010}. Because the conserved variables are
defined on the map plane, the geometric quantities needed by this law are
introduced explicitly. Let
\begin{equation}
  U_h=\sqrt{u_a^2+v_a^2},\qquad
  \tan\phi=\lVert\nabla B\rVert,\qquad
  \tan\psi=-\frac{(u_a,v_a)\boldsymbol{\cdot}\nabla B}{U_h},
  \label{eq:avac-geometry}
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ is the local bed angle and $\psi$ is the inclination of the
velocity in its instantaneous direction. Thus the terrain-normal depth and
terrain-tangent speed represented by the Cartesian state are
$h_n=h_a\cos\phi$ and $U=U_h/\cos\psi$, respectively. The curvature sampled
by motion in that direction is the projection of the fixed-bed Hessian,
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_v=
  \frac{u_a^2B_{xx}+2u_av_aB_{xy}+v_a^2B_{yy}}{U_h^2}.
  \label{eq:avac-curvature}
\end{equation}
For the scalar basal source, AVAC evaluates $B_x$, $B_y$, $B_{xx}$, and $B_{yy}$
with centred differences and $B_{xy}$ with a four-point diagonal stencil.
The transport operator has the domain-aware boundary treatment described
below. No curvature smoothing is applied. The constitutive basal stress combines a Coulomb contribution,
cohesion, and Voellmy resistance,
\begin{equation}
  \tau_b=\mu\sigma_b+I_C C+I_V\frac{\rho_a g}{\xi}U^2,
  \label{eq:avac-rheology}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_a$ is avalanche bulk density, $\mu$ the Coulomb coefficient,
$\xi$ the Voellmy turbulent coefficient, and $C$ cohesion. The indicators are
$(I_C,I_V)=(0,0)$ for Coulomb, $(0,1)$ for Voellmy, and $(1,1)$ for cohesive
Voellmy. The code does not evolve $\sigma_b$ as an independent variable; its
gravitational and centripetal contributions enter the projected Coulomb terms
in Eqs.~\eqref{eq:avac-source-coefficients} and
\eqref{eq:avac-curvature-normal-load}.

The Cartesian steep-slope correction, curvature-dependent normal load, and
basal stress are applied together. Projecting the large-slope gravity
correction onto the flow direction, as proposed by
\citet{HergartenRobl2015}, gives
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{S}_r=-h_a\left(a+bU_h^2\right)
  \frac{(u_a,v_a)}{U_h}, \qquad U_h>0,
  \label{eq:avac-friction-vector}
\end{equation}
with
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  a={}&g\sin^2\phi\tan\psi
       +\mu g\cos\phi\cos\psi
       +I_C\frac{C\cos\psi}{\rho_a h_a\cos\phi},\\
  b={}&I_V\frac{g}{\xi h_a\cos\phi\cos\psi}
       +\mu\kappa_v\cos\phi\cos\psi.
  \end{split}
  \label{eq:avac-source-coefficients}
\end{equation}
The first contribution to $a$ removes the excessive flow-parallel component
of Cartesian shallow-water gravity; the remaining terms are the projected
Coulomb, cohesive, and turbulent resistances. Curvature changes $b$ through
$\mu\kappa_v\cos\phi\cos\psi$, the centripetal contribution to Coulomb
normal load: concave curvature increases the load, whereas convex curvature
decreases it \citep{FischerEtAl2012}. The curvature-dependent normal-load
correction is distinct from transport of a velocity vector between changing
terrain tangent planes. AVAC applies a separate finite rotation after the
conservative flux update and dry-state cleanup, before the scalar basal
source. Let $\boldsymbol{u}^{*}$ be the provisional horizontal velocity,
$\boldsymbol{b}_a=\nabla B$ the arrival gradient, and $H_B$ the local Hessian.
A first-order material backtrace estimates the departure gradient as
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \boldsymbol{b}_d&=\boldsymbol{b}_a-\Delta t\,H_B\boldsymbol{u}^{*},\\
    \boldsymbol{n}_k&=\frac{(-\boldsymbol{b}_k,1)}
    {\sqrt{1+\lVert\boldsymbol{b}_k\rVert^2}},\quad k\in\{d,a\}.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:avac-transport-normals}
\end{equation}
The provisional velocity is lifted to the departure tangent plane as
$\boldsymbol{w}_d=(\boldsymbol{u}^{*},
\boldsymbol{b}_d\boldsymbol{\cdot}\boldsymbol{u}^{*})$.
Writing $\boldsymbol{s}=(\boldsymbol{n}_d+\boldsymbol{n}_a)/
\lVert\boldsymbol{n}_d+\boldsymbol{n}_a\rVert$, the implementation uses two
orthogonal reflections to obtain the minimal rotation,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \boldsymbol{w}_a&=
    (I-2\boldsymbol{s}\boldsymbol{s}^{\mathsf T})
    (I-2\boldsymbol{n}_d\boldsymbol{n}_d^{\mathsf T})\boldsymbol{w}_d,\\
    (m_{x,a},m_{y,a})^{\dagger}&=h_a(w_{a,x},w_{a,y}).
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:avac-tangent-transport}
\end{equation}
Both graph normals point upward, so their bisector is defined for finite
slopes. The rotation preserves the norm of the \emph{lifted departure}
velocity and changes neither depth nor mass. Its differential horizontal
acceleration is
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{S}_g/h_a=
  -\frac{\nabla B}{1+\lVert\nabla B\rVert^2}
  \left(\boldsymbol{u}^{\mathsf T}H_B\boldsymbol{u}\right).
  \label{eq:avac-geometric-source}
\end{equation}
Using finite normals avoids integrating this connection acceleration as a
frozen quadratic growth law. The operation is an exact identity on an affine
bed and at zero velocity; it is inactive for numerically dry cells and for
the water model.

Transport derivatives use genuine neighbouring topography across internal
patch boundaries, including periodic ghosts. At a physical exterior, the
nearest wholly physical quadratic stencil supplies a gradient extrapolated
to the cell centre. A direction with only two physical samples resolves its
slope but not pure curvature; one sample implies extrusion. Mixed derivatives
are retained when both directions have at least two samples. An affine-bed
roundoff test avoids reconstructing momentum when all second differences
are below the scale-aware tolerance specified below.

This is a split, local approximation using the post-flux velocity, not an
exact reconstruction of all material paths entering a finite-volume cell.
The preserved lifted-vector norm is not a proof of energy conservation for
the complete spatial update. The hydrostatic pressure flux remains Cartesian
and isotropic, and the steep-slope gravity correction remains flow-parallel.
Thus the added transport does not constitute a fully surface-metric pressure
and momentum discretization or a complete surface-fitted granular model.

The curvature-dependent Coulomb contribution to the speed equation can be
written compactly as
\begin{equation}
  -\mu\cos\phi\cos\psi\left(g+\kappa_v U_h^2\right).
  \label{eq:avac-curvature-normal-load}
\end{equation}
On convex terrain, $\kappa_v<0$, the quantity in parentheses vanishes at
\begin{equation}
  U_{h,c}=\sqrt{-g/\kappa_v}.
  \label{eq:avac-contact-speed}
\end{equation}
AVAC does not allow this effective Coulomb normal load to become tensile, a
limiting choice also documented among the curvature treatments compared by
\citet{WirbelEtAl2026}.
Below $U_{h,c}$ it integrates the contact branch; above it, the gravity and
curvature parts of the Coulomb contribution are removed together. If a source
step crosses the threshold, the crossing time is located with 60 bisection
iterations and the remainder is integrated on the other branch. Voellmy drag,
cohesion, and the large-slope gravity correction remain active. This is a
no-tensile-normal-load approximation rather than a ballistic detachment
model: AVAC does not introduce an airborne phase. The
retention of cohesion outside the Coulomb-contact branch is an explicit
constitutive choice of the present implementation.

On a plane with motion directly downslope, $\psi=\phi$. Combining
Eq.~\eqref{eq:avac-source-coefficients} with GeoClaw's map-plane driving term
and converting from $U_h$ to $U$ recovers
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm dU}{\mathrm dt}
  =g\left(\sin\phi-\mu\cos\phi
   -I_C\frac{C}{\rho_a g h_n}
   -I_V\frac{U^2}{\xi h_n}\right),
  \label{eq:avac-planar-voellmy}
\end{equation}
and, for the non-cohesive Voellmy model,
$U_\infty=[\xi h_n(\sin\phi-\mu\cos\phi)]^{1/2}$. This planar limit is used as
a source-level verification of the implementation.

During one operator-split \emph{scalar basal-source} step, $h_a$, the local
bed gradient and Hessian, the altitude-selected rheological parameters, and
the map-plane velocity direction are held fixed.
Equations~\eqref{eq:avac-friction-vector}
and~\eqref{eq:avac-source-coefficients} then reduce to
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm d U_h}{\mathrm dt}=-a-bU_h^2.
  \label{eq:avac-speed-ode}
\end{equation}
AVAC integrates this local equation analytically. For $a>0$ and $b>0$,
\begin{equation}
  U_h^{n+1}=
  \begin{cases}
  \displaystyle
  \sqrt{\frac{a}{b}}\tan\!\left[
  \tan^{-1}\!\left(U_h^n\sqrt{\frac{b}{a}}\right)
  -\sqrt{ab}\,\Delta t\right], & \Phi>0,\\[6pt]
  0, & \Phi\leq0,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:avac-friction-update}
\end{equation}
where $\Phi$ denotes the phase in square brackets. The limiting cases are
$U_h^{n+1}=\max(0,U_h^n-a\Delta t)$ for $b=0$ and
$U_h^{n+1}=U_h^n/(1+bU_h^n\Delta t)$ for $a=0$. Uphill motion can give $a<0$
because the correction then returns part of GeoClaw's excessive opposing
gravity; the corresponding exact hyperbolic branch tends to
$\sqrt{-a/b}$ rather than forcing a false stop. While the analytical speed is
positive, the two momenta are scaled by $U_h^{n+1}/U_h^n$, preserving their
map-plane direction. For Voellmy and cohesive Voellmy, a zero scalar result
always sets both momenta to zero: no direction must be assigned to a zero
vector. This can be a transient source-stage stop on a super-yield bed; the
subsequent Riemann update still supplies pressure and bed-slope driving, while
static equilibrium additionally requires the yield conditions below.
Discarding the integrated impulse merely because the bed is super-yield
would incorrectly remove the stiff $1/h_a$ Voellmy drag precisely when a
source step reaches zero.

Pure Coulomb retains its separate compatibility rule: a zero scalar result
sets momentum to zero only if Eq.~\eqref{eq:avac-cell-yield} supports static
rest; otherwise the incoming momentum is retained for that source substep.
This is a legacy split-step fallback, not an exact continuation of the scalar
ODE through zero. Consequently, although the constitutive turbulent term
vanishes as $\xi\to\infty$, the two caller-level stopping policies need not
coincide in that limit. No empirical speed floor or locally assigned reversal
is introduced.

The closed-form impulse eliminates the explicit time-discretization error
of the frozen scalar drag problem. The implementation also uses
the analytical trigonometric, hyperbolic, linear, or rational branch required
by all other sign combinations of $a$ and $b$. For admissible non-negative
rheological parameters, a $b<0$ contact state can arise only from convex
curvature; the contact-limited update transitions at
Eq.~\eqref{eq:avac-contact-speed} before that branch can become the returned
momentum state. Exact integration of this frozen local subproblem does not
make the flux--transport--source composition exact or independent of the
time step: depth, velocity direction, geometry sampled by motion, and driving
forces evolve between substeps. Timestep refinement therefore remains a
separate numerical verification requirement.

Users may assign piecewise-constant values of $\mu$, $\xi$, and $C$ to
altitude intervals; the moving rheological source selects the active interval
from the bed elevation at the cell centre. The static Riemann checks instead
select one set of parameters from the mean bed elevation at each interface,
which is an intentional but discontinuous approximation at an altitude-zone
boundary. The rheological source is enabled to a nominal depth of
$10^6$~m and therefore acts at every physically relevant avalanche depth.
The angles in Eq.~\eqref{eq:avac-geometry} are computed from the
fixed bed rather than the evolving free surface. This is the
\textit{Gerris-$z_b$} choice of \citet{HergartenRobl2015}; it avoids a second,
inconsistent numerical differentiation of $h_a+B$. The implementation
includes the curvature-dependent normal-load and steep-slope corrections
projected along the instantaneous map-plane velocity. Although $B_{xy}$ enters
both the directional curvature $\kappa_v$ and the separate tangent transport,
AVAC includes no surface-fitted transverse pressure or active--passive
earth-pressure closure. The Riemann-interface arrest
criterion described below also remains a map-plane approximation. Because the
fixed DEM is differentiated without smoothing, the curvature normal-load
source inherits sensitivity to DEM resolution and short-wavelength elevation
noise.

\subsubsection{Static yield, stopping, and arrested depth}

Kinetic resistance alone is insufficient to retain a non-horizontal arrested
layer:
the pressure and bed-slope terms can remobilize a cell after its velocity has
reached zero. AVAC therefore adds a set-valued static-yield treatment inspired
by the arrest strategy used in D-Claw \citep{GeorgeIverson2014}. At the cell
level the terrain angle is evaluated from centred DEM gradients. A cell that
is exactly at rest remains at rest when
\begin{equation}
  g h_a\tan\theta
  \leq \mu g h_a + I_C\frac{C}{\rho_a\cos^2\theta}.
  \label{eq:avac-cell-yield}
\end{equation}
For a moving cell, the analytical update of Eq.~\eqref{eq:avac-speed-ode}
determines the source-stage speed. Voellmy and cohesive Voellmy honour a
zero result even on a super-yield slope; this transient stop is not a claim
that the static inequality holds. Only pure Coulomb retains the incoming
momentum when a zero result occurs on a super-yield slope. Subsequent
pressure and bed-slope driving remain part of the coupled flux update.

A second check acts in the normal Riemann solver to prevent pressure-driven
creep through interfaces within an arrested deposit. For a wet--wet interface
of spacing $\Delta n$, the directional threshold is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta\eta_y
  =\left(\mu+I_C\frac{C}
  {\rho_a g\bar h_a\cos^2\theta_n}\right)\Delta n,
  \qquad \eta_a=h_a+B,
  \label{eq:avac-interface-yield}
\end{equation}
where $\bar h_a$ is the mean interface depth and $\theta_n$ is the bed angle
estimated from the elevation difference normal to that interface. Before this
directional test can suppress waves, each eligible wet cell must also satisfy
the cell-centred map-plane condition
\begin{equation}
 R_{ij}=
 \frac{\left\lVert\nabla_c\eta_a\right\rVert_2}
 {\displaystyle \mu_{ij}+I_C\frac{C_{ij}}
 {\rho_a g h_{a,ij}\cos^2\theta_{B,ij}}}
 \leq 1,\qquad
 \cos^2\theta_{B,ij}=
 \frac{1}{1+B_{x,c,ij}^2+B_{y,c,ij}^2}.
 \label{eq:avac-vector-interface-yield}
\end{equation}
Here $\nabla_c$, $B_{x,c}$, and $B_{y,c}$ are centred differences over the
four neighbouring cell centres, and $\mu_{ij}$ and $C_{ij}$ are selected from
the bed elevation of that cell. The ratio is refreshed in a transient
cell-centred auxiliary field before the directional Riemann sweeps. It blocks
static suppression when a diagonal free-surface gradient is vector
super-yield even though each normal increment considered separately is
sub-yield.

The flux is therefore suppressed only when the available wet--wet stencil is
exactly at rest, its wet cells have $R_{ij}\leq1$, and all three of the actual
adjacent head jump $|\eta_{a,R}-\eta_{a,L}|$, a minmod-limited local increment,
and the corresponding stencil-scale increment of $\eta_a$ do not exceed
$\Delta\eta_y$. The actual jump is necessary because limited or centred
differences can cancel across a plateau edge or alternating cell pattern.
On a flat, non-cohesive bed its condition follows from
$(g/2)|h_R^2-h_L^2|\leq\mu g\bar h_a\Delta n$, equivalently
$|h_R-h_L|\leq\mu\Delta n$. The code also evaluates a local closed-form
basal stopping predictor over the trial step, with coefficients
$a_p=\mu g+I_C C/(\rho_a h_a)$ and $b_p=I_V g/(\xi h_a)$.
It does not use a predicted future stop to suppress a currently moving
interface: exact rest is required independently. This predictor is not the
full curved-terrain source update or a source-stage CFL bound. Where the
four-cell stencil is unavailable at a
patch/stencil edge, the implementation falls back to the two interface states;
an unavailable or invalid vector marker disables static suppression rather
than applying a component-wise fallback. At an interface between an arrested
wet cell and a dry cell, the same directional expression is evaluated with the
wet-cell depth and the head increment
$\max(0,\eta_{a,\mathrm{wet}}-B_{\mathrm{dry}})$, and the adjacent wet cell
must meet Eq.~\eqref{eq:avac-vector-interface-yield}. Thus cohesion is
included at both wet--wet and wet--dry interfaces. These conditions preserve
an admissible deposit without averaging its free surface. The normal interface
threshold uses parameters selected from the mean adjacent bed elevation,
whereas the vector eligibility check uses the local cell values. Both use
interface/centred bed differences rather than the Hessian-based moving source,
and hence remain map-plane approximations of static yield. The new vector
gate is necessary for static arrest, but not a monolithic rotationally
invariant two-dimensional complementarity solve: the normal interface tests
remain direction-dependent, even though the wave-propagation update includes
transverse corrections. States that fail these eligibility checks are allowed
to evolve through the Riemann solver.

No empirical stopping velocity is used. Depths at or below the numerical dry
tolerance have their momenta set to zero, and the unresolved wet--dry fringe
is regularized as described below. At resolved depths, the transition from
motion to rest follows the analytical source impulse and the static-yield
conditions, with the separate pure-Coulomb fallback and Voellmy transient
stops stated above. This exact-zero
construction for statically supportable states is the feature assessed by the
deposit and steady-slope tests in Sect.~\ref{sec:avac-verification}. The input
field historically named $u_{\mathrm{cr}}$ is retained in the configuration
schema for compatibility but is not used by the present solver.

\subsubsection{Finite-volume solution and adaptive refinement}

The homogeneous and topographic parts of
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:avac-mass}--\eqref{eq:avac-ymomentum} are advanced with
GeoClaw's augmented $f$-wave Riemann solver, which is well balanced for steady
water surfaces and explicitly resolves inundation over variable topography
\citep{George2008}. AVAC replaces the standard normal solver only to add the
static-yield decisions above; it retains the three-wave, second-order,
unsplit wave-propagation update and transverse corrections. A single
user-selected TVD limiter is applied to the three wave families. A newly
selected Coulomb model defaults to Minmod in the plugin, whereas Voellmy and
cohesive Voellmy retain Superbee; saved explicit choices are preserved.
The ISeeSnow protocol instead specifies Van Leer for its two Voellmy cases
and Minmod for Coulomb. These benchmark choices are not substituted silently
for the general plugin defaults.

At moving wet--dry margins, division of momentum by a vanishing cell-average
depth can otherwise create a large, non-physical velocity while leaving the
conserved momentum small. The Riemann solver itself uses the conservative
ratio $(u_a,v_a)=(m_{x,a},m_{y,a})/h_a$ whenever
$h_a>h_{\mathrm{dry}}$. AVAC does not project or relax cell-average momentum
after each source step. Instead, on locally non-planar terrain it tapers the
conservative high-order correction flux at an interface. For $h_\epsilon>0$,
let $h_{\min}=\max[0,\min(h_L,h_R)]$, $r=h_{\min}/h_\epsilon$, and let
$\chi_f=1$ when either neighbouring cell is classified as non-planar. The
correction factor is
\begin{equation}
  R_f=\begin{cases}
    \displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2}\,r^2}{\sqrt{1+r^4}},
       & h_{\min}<h_\epsilon\text{ and }\chi_f=1,\\[5pt]
    1, & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:avac-correction-taper}
\end{equation}
Zero $h_\epsilon$ disables this taper. The default depths are
$h_\epsilon=\SI{0.05}{\metre}$ for Coulomb and
$\SI{0.10}{\metre}$ for both Voellmy laws; they are independent of mesh
spacing and the reporting threshold $h_v$. The same factor multiplies all
three components of each high-order correction and is retained when that
correction is split transversely. The first-order normal flux, its Riemann
states and its wave-speed calculation are unchanged. The usual $\Delta t/
\Delta x$ and $\Delta t/\Delta y$ factors remain in the conservative update,
rather than accumulating an extra momentum multiplier once per source step.
The factor has the shape of the velocity desingularization of
\citet{KurganovPetrova2007}, but its use on correction fluxes and its terrain
trigger are AVAC-specific numerical choices, not a new physical basal law.

The two-dimensional terrain mask includes pure and mixed second differences.
For stencil centre $B_c$, let $D_2$ be their largest absolute value,
$A_B=\max(1,\max|B|)$ and $L_B=\max|B-B_c|$, with elevations in metres.
A stencil is non-planar when
\begin{equation}
  D_2>\max\!\left[10^{-12}\max(1,L_B),\,
                 64\epsilon_{\mathrm{mach}}A_B\right].
  \label{eq:avac-nonplanarity}
\end{equation}
This auxiliary mask is refreshed before the Riemann sweeps, including the
extended ghost stencil used by the positivity relimiter. It trusts internal
and periodic ghost terrain and excludes only exterior physical closures,
using the same domain-aware stencil convention as the transport operator.
An affine discrete bed below this roundoff threshold bypasses the taper;
that property is not a guarantee that every AMR representation of an
analytically planar DEM remains below the threshold after topographic
integration. Water does not use the granular mask or correction taper.

Donor-based positivity relimiting is enabled for granular runs. Available
cell depth bounds outgoing face fluxes, with the same positive scaling for
all conserved components and the two-sided source-related flux differences
retained. Face ownership is determined before any component is modified,
and the extended normal and transverse stencils are evaluated consistently.
The flux-based operation is distinct from a post-update transfer of material
between neighbouring cells: AVAC performs no such deficit redistribution.
Residual negative depths are clipped to zero after the flux update, and
numerically dry cells have zero momentum. Such clipping can add a residual
volume, so conservation must still be measured rather than inferred from
the relimiter alone. The default dry tolerance is
$h_{\mathrm{dry}}=\SI{1e-4}{\metre}$.

Peak diagnostics apply a separate, non-invasive depth rule. Depth is copied
from the non-negative current state without applying the velocity threshold;
reported velocity is zero for $h_a\leq h_v$, follows
the desingularization
\begin{equation}
  \widehat u_a=\frac{\sqrt{2}\,h_a m_{x,a}}
  {\left[h_a^4+\max(h_a^4,(4h_v)^4)\right]^{1/2}},
  \qquad
  \widehat v_a=\frac{\sqrt{2}\,h_a m_{y,a}}
  {\left[h_a^4+\max(h_a^4,(4h_v)^4)\right]^{1/2}}
  \label{eq:avac-state-regularization}
\end{equation}
for
$h_v<h_a<4h_v$, and is exactly momentum divided by depth for
$h_a\geq4h_v$. The default $h_v$ is \SI{0.05}{\metre}, so the diagnostic
transition ends at \SI{0.20}{\metre}. The horizontal diagnostic velocity is
converted to the terrain-tangent magnitude using $w_a=u_aB_x+v_aB_y$ before
the temporal maximum is updated. This reporting operation does not alter the
conserved state. AVAC writes the shared GeoClaw speed guard as the deliberately
nonphysical value $10^{99}$~\si{\metre\per\second}; this is not an operational
finite speed cap.

Topographic balance is incorporated in the augmented $f$-wave Riemann solve.
After the flux update and dry-state cleanup, terrain-tangent transport is
applied once, followed by the scalar granular rheological source, using
first-order Godunov splitting. Second-order spatial reconstruction therefore
does not imply a second-order accurate full source-split method.

Before committing an AMR-level step, a CFL preflight repeats the preparation
and normal Riemann sweeps on private copies of the incoming depth, momentum
and auxiliary fields. Its wave-speed reduction matches that of the physical
flux update. If the maximum trial Courant number $C_{\mathrm{trial}}$ exceeds
the configured ceiling $C_{\max}$, the common level step is reduced toward
\begin{equation}
  \Delta t_{\mathrm{retry}}=
  \Delta t\,\frac{\min(C_{\mathrm{target}},0.9C_{\max})}
                    {C_{\mathrm{trial}}},
  \label{eq:avac-cfl-retry}
\end{equation}
and the trial is repeated. No rejected trial commits solution fields, flux
registers, clocks, gauges or peak observations. The accepted physical update
then executes once. A non-finite trial CFL, invalid timestep or CFL settings,
a non-advancing time step, an excessive fixed step, or more than 20 rejected trials cause an explicit
failure. This is an incoming-wave CFL test, not a full post-flux or
post-source state acceptance test. It neither bounds every shallow-cell
velocity nor proves stability or convergence of the split scheme.

The plugin defaults are $C_{\mathrm{target}}=0.5$ and $C_{\max}=1$.
The ISeeSnow Coulomb protocol explicitly uses target 0.25, while the two
Voellmy cases use 0.5, all with ceiling 1. A halved target need not halve
every accepted step, because wave speeds and output/AMR synchronization also
influence the selected time step.

Ordinary granular runs use five ghost-cell layers; water uses two.
The synthetic Coulomb analytical harness can explicitly request a restricted
two-layer compatibility configuration for its narrow strips. Real-terrain runs use a one-way,
zero-gradient outflow boundary:
an outward state is copied while attempted inward normal momentum is reflected
to block external avalanche inflow. Verification problems may instead select
the boundary condition required by their analytical solution. Wetting and
drying follow GeoClaw's depth tolerance, supplemented by the AVAC arrested-edge
criterion.

AVAC can use patch-based adaptive mesh refinement following the Clawpack
wave-propagation framework \citep{BergerLeVeque1998,BergerEtAl2011}. The plugin
disables the usual free-surface wave-height flag and uses the following
automatic velocity-based flag for a wet cell when its speed exceeds the
level-specific tolerance and its kinetic
proxy satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \sqrt{h_a}\,U_h>\sqrt{h_v}\,U_{\mathrm{tol}}.
  \label{eq:avac-amr-flag}
\end{equation}
The second condition prevents a vanishing-depth numerical tail from creating
fine patches, and it is independent of the disabled velocity ceiling. Explicit
AMR regions can also require refinement of static or dry terrain. The current
plugin supplies \SI{0.05}{\metre\per\second} as the speed tolerance for every
requested level transition, regrids periodically, and subcycles finer levels
in time. When a fine-level CFL retry shortens the admissible step, the
remaining parent interval is repartitioned into an integer number of equal
substeps so that synchronization is retained. Counts are checked for finite
values, integer overflow and representable time advancement; no arbitrary
100-substep ceiling replaces the numerical timestep constraint. The bundled
plugin template defaults to one refinement level, and the ISeeSnow comparisons
reported here also use one level; the analytical AMR tests use explicitly
different level and stencil settings.

During coarse-to-fine transfer, AVAC reconstructs mobile depth rather than a
lake-at-rest free surface because $h_a$ is avalanche mass per unit map area.
Minmod-limited depth slopes in the two coordinate directions are scaled
together, when necessary, so that every child depth is non-negative while the
parent mean is retained. During fine-to-coarse restriction, depth and both
momenta are averaged over all child cells, including dry children; averaging
only wet cells would duplicate mass at a partly wet front. A deliberately low
sea-level value of $-10^4$~m prevents standard GeoClaw initialization of a new
fine patch from creating water-like depth over below-datum dry topography.
Together with release mapping and the exact frozen-coefficient friction
impulse, these transfers make avalanche volume the primary AMR invariant,
subject to the residual clipping possible in the post-flux positivity repair.
The round-trip terrain writer and affine-bypass tests do not establish
exact planar preservation under every AMR topographic integration: this
remaining geometric conditioning issue is separate from the conservative
depth and momentum transfer rules.



\subsection{WAVE governing equations and numerical method}
\label{sec:wave-method}

\subsubsection{Shallow-water equations}

WAVE uses the standard GeoClaw state
$\boldsymbol{q}_w=(h_w,h_wu_w,h_wv_w)^{\mathsf T}$, where $h_w$ is water depth
and $(u_w,v_w)$ is the horizontal depth-averaged water velocity. Away from the
AVAC source it solves
\begin{align}
  \partial_t h_w
    +\partial_x(h_w u_w)+\partial_y(h_w v_w) &=0,
    \label{eq:wave-mass}\\
  \partial_t(h_wu_w)
    +\partial_x\!\left(h_wu_w^2+\tfrac12gh_w^2\right)
    +\partial_y(h_wu_wv_w)
    &=-gh_w\,\partial_xB_w+S_{x,M},
    \label{eq:wave-xmomentum}\\
  \partial_t(h_wv_w)
    +\partial_x(h_wu_wv_w)
    +\partial_y\!\left(h_wv_w^2+\tfrac12gh_w^2\right)
    &=-gh_w\,\partial_yB_w+S_{y,M},
    \label{eq:wave-ymomentum}
\end{align}
where $B_w$ is the terrain/bathymetry surface prepared for WAVE. It is read
from a separately selected DEM that covers the AVAC rectangle in the same
coordinate reference system and therefore need not equal the fixed AVAC bed
$B$. Both standard configurations use $g=\SI{9.81}{\metre\per\second\squared}$.
$\boldsymbol{S}_M$ is Manning resistance. The coupling source introduced in
Sect.~\ref{sec:coupling-method} is added to all three equations at shoreline
cells.

These nonlinear shallow-water equations assume hydrostatic pressure and omit
frequency dispersion. They are appropriate when the relevant wavelength is
large relative to water depth, but they cannot resolve the three-dimensional,
non-hydrostatic impact zone. This limitation is especially relevant to
landslide- and avalanche-generated waves \citep{PoulainEtAl2023}; the purpose
of the present coupling is to prescribe depth-integrated mass and momentum to
a long-wave propagation model, not to reproduce grain-scale impact physics.

\subsubsection{Initial lake, friction, and wetting/drying}

Let $\zeta_0$ be the absolute initial lake level. Before shoreline
stabilization, the desired lake-at-rest state is
\begin{equation}
  h_w(x,y,0)=
  \begin{cases}
    \max(\zeta_0-B_w(x,y),0), & (x,y)\in\Omega_L,\\
    0, & (x,y)\notin\Omega_L,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad u_w=v_w=0,
  \label{eq:wave-initial-lake}
\end{equation}
where $\Omega_L$ is the lake polygon rasterized on the WAVE mesh. When GDAL
rasterization is available, the polygon uses an all-touched rule at the
terrain resolution; the dependency-free fallback uses centre-point inclusion
instead. If the WAVE grid is coarser, the fine binary mask is aggregated and
a WAVE cell belongs to the lake when at least half of its fine mask cells are
inside. This is an area approximation that prevents a narrow polygon sliver
from turning an entire coarse cell into water.

GeoClaw cell-averages a piecewise-linear topographic representation. A dry
cell beside a deep lake can therefore acquire a mean bed below $\zeta_0$ even
when its centre lies outside $\Omega_L$, producing a numerical wave when the
initial dry mask is released. AVAC4QGIS avoids that artifact by raising every
exterior terrain sample below the waterline, and---when at least one wet
interior cell remains---an eight-neighbour one-cell inner rim, to
$\zeta_0+h_{\mathrm{dry}}$. The force-dry file is configured through
$t_0+\SI{0.5}{\second}$ when initial or newly refined patches are constructed;
in the standard one-level WAVE workflow it therefore acts only at
initialization. The stabilized rim is not a permanent wall: subsequent inflow
or runup can wet a cell once its computed water surface exceeds the guarded
bed elevation.

WAVE expresses bed friction through Strickler coefficients $K=1/n$, where $n$
is the Manning coefficient. With
$\boldsymbol{m}_w=(h_wu_w,h_wv_w)$, the implemented local source update is
\begin{equation}
  \gamma_M=
  \frac{g n^2\lVert\boldsymbol{m}_w\rVert}{h_w^{7/3}},
  \qquad
  \boldsymbol{m}_w^{n+1}
  =\frac{\boldsymbol{m}_w^n}{1+\gamma_M\Delta t}.
  \label{eq:wave-manning}
\end{equation}
It is applied only where $h_w$ is above the dry tolerance and below the
configured friction-depth limit. Two coefficients can be separated by the
initial water elevation. Their executable mapping is
\begin{equation}
  n(B_w)=
  \begin{cases}
    1/K_w, & B_w<\zeta_0,\\
    1/K_l, & B_w\geq\zeta_0,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:wave-strickler-zones}
\end{equation}
where $K_w$ and $K_l$ are the water and land Strickler coefficients,
respectively. AVAC4QGIS writes them in this order because GeoClaw applies its
first coefficient below the elevation break and its second coefficient above
the break.

Because the coupled signal can be smaller than an imbalance created during
lake preparation, the unforced lake is itself a model test. A stationary
lake, stable wet--dry edge, and negligible drift in water volume are required
before an avalanche source is interpreted; these properties are evaluated in
Sect.~\ref{sec:wave-verification}.

\subsubsection{Numerical solution and adaptive refinement}

WAVE uses GeoClaw's unmodified augmented normal Riemann solver and transverse
solver \citep{George2008}. As in AVAC, the finite-volume update is
second-order, unsplit, and based on three limited $f$-waves; topographic
balance is embedded in the augmented $f$-wave solve, while the Manning and
injected shoreline sources are applied by Godunov splitting. The time step is
adapted to user-specified target and maximum Courant numbers. The standard configuration uses
zero-order extrapolation at the four outer boundaries, while analytical tests
can select periodic or wall boundaries when required. Dry states, shoreline
motion, and inundation use the standard GeoClaw wetting-and-drying algorithm
\citep{BergerEtAl2011}.

The underlying solver and the internal shoreline source are compatible with
patch-based AMR because total source rates are divided by the area of the
receiving patch cell. However, the normal AVAC4QGIS WAVE workflow in the
version documented here deliberately prepares one refinement level. Thus the
reported plugin simulations use a uniform WAVE grid, whereas AMR capability
remains a property to be activated and evaluated separately. Verification is
still required for the plugin-specific lake mask, shoreline stabilization,
and source routine even though the homogeneous water solver is established
GeoClaw software.


\subsection{One-way AVAC-to-WAVE shoreline coupling}
\label{sec:coupling-method}

\subsubsection{Lake-mask and shoreline construction}

The coupling follows the long-standing idea of representing avalanche entry
as a forcing of a depth-averaged reservoir model \citep{Vila1987,Ancey2023}.
AVAC4QGIS makes that forcing spatially and temporally explicit on the WAVE
mesh. The lake polygon defines the initially wet body $\Omega_L$, not the
rectangular calculation domain. Every cell in the polygon-derived WAVE mask
adjacent to a cell outside that mask contributes one grid-aligned shoreline
face. Such a target can be a zero-depth cell of the numerical stabilization
rim rather than an initially wet cell. For each face $f$, the preprocessing
stores the face centre, the centre of the source-target cell, the face length
$L_f$, and the unit normal
$\boldsymbol{n}_f=(n_{x,f},n_{y,f})$ pointing from land into the lake.

This construction produces a staircase approximation of the polygon at the
chosen WAVE resolution. A mask corner cell can therefore contribute two faces,
and all of their fluxes are retained. The shoreline resolution is controlled
by WAVE cell size rather than by an independent number of sampling points,
which keeps the geometric flux integral and its receiving cells in the same
discretization. The polygon should retain at least one dry WAVE-cell margin to
the rectangular domain boundary: a missing exterior neighbour is currently
treated as dry and would otherwise create an outer-boundary source face.

\subsubsection{Sampling the AVAC state}

At every saved AVAC time, the three conserved components are interpolated at
the WAVE shoreline-face centres. Bilinear interpolation is performed within
each AVAC patch, with finite values on finer patches replacing coarser ones.
Samples outside every AVAC patch are recorded and replaced by a zero state;
the workflow fails only if every shoreline sample is outside AVAC coverage in
every saved frame.
Denoting avalanche depth and map-grid velocity by
$h_a$ and $(u_a,v_a)$, respectively, the inward normal volume flux per unit
shoreline length is
\begin{equation}
  q_{n,f} = (h_a u_a)_f n_{x,f} + (h_a v_a)_f n_{y,f}.
  \label{eq:normal-flux}
\end{equation}
Only finite samples with $h_a>\epsilon_h$ and $q_{n,f}>\epsilon_q$ contribute
to the WAVE source. The current implementation assigns the same numerical
tolerance to $\epsilon_h$ and $\epsilon_q$ in their respective units. The
positive-flux condition admits avalanche material entering the lake but does
not withdraw water when the sampled avalanche velocity points out of it.

\subsubsection{Transferred volume and horizontal momentum}

For a shoreline face of length $L_f$, AVAC4QGIS constructs the water-volume
rate
\begin{equation}
  Q_{w,f} = \beta q_{n,f} L_f,
  \label{eq:water-volume-rate}
\end{equation}
and supplies WAVE with the three conservative rates
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{R}_f =
  \left(Q_{w,f},\;Q_{w,f}u_{a,f},\;Q_{w,f}v_{a,f}\right)^{\mathsf T}.
  \label{eq:coupling-source}
\end{equation}
The units of the three entries are \si{\metre\cubed\per\second},
\si{\metre\tothe{4}\per\second\squared}, and
\si{\metre\tothe{4}\per\second\squared}, respectively. Multiplication of
the last two time integrals by water density converts them into physical
horizontal momentum.

The coefficient $\beta$ combines the snow-to-water volume conversion and any
unresolved transfer efficiency. It can be written as
\begin{equation}
  \beta=\varepsilon_T\frac{\rho_a}{\rho_w},
  \label{eq:coupling-beta}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_w$ is water density and $\varepsilon_T$ is a dimensionless
efficiency. For $\varepsilon_T=1$, the avalanche mass rate
$\rho_a q_{n,f}L_f$ equals the injected water mass rate
$\rho_wQ_{w,f}$, and the two horizontal momentum rates also agree. For
$\varepsilon_T<1$, both transferred mass and momentum are reduced by the same
factor. The current interface exposes their product $\beta$ as a single
``snow-to-water damping'' parameter; mass and momentum conservation are
therefore exact only when that parameter is set to $\rho_a/\rho_w$. The QGIS
control supplies $\beta$ directly under the name ``damping'' and constrains it
to $0\leq\beta\leq0.4$.

This density dependence is not cosmetic. Laboratory work on snow-avalanche
impulse waves shows that buoyancy, impact velocity, and the density ratio
control momentum and energy transfer \citep{ZittiEtAl2016}. The present
operator is a deliberately reduced closure: it preserves the incoming
depth-integrated horizontal quantities when
Eq.~\eqref{eq:coupling-beta} is conservative, but it does not resolve the
underlying two-phase interaction. In particular, the operator transfers no
energy, vertical momentum, granular-volume removal, or pressure impulse.

\subsubsection{Temporal interpolation and injection into WAVE}

Equation~\eqref{eq:coupling-source} is evaluated at every saved AVAC output
time. Contributions from faces that point to the same WAVE source-target cell
are summed, producing a piecewise-linear time series
$\boldsymbol{R}_c(t)$ for each source cell $c$. During an accepted WAVE step
from $t$ to $t+\Delta t$, the source routine samples that series at the
midpoint and applies
\begin{equation}
  \boldsymbol{q}_{w,c}^{*}
  =\boldsymbol{q}_{w,c}^{n}
   +\frac{\Delta t}{A_c}\,
    \boldsymbol{R}_c\!\left(t+\tfrac12\Delta t\right),
  \label{eq:wave-source-update}
\end{equation}
where $A_c$ is the area of the WAVE patch cell that receives the source. The
Manning update follows within the same split source step. Because the stored
quantities are total rates and division uses the actual receiving area, the
integrated injection is independent of cell area and remains well defined if
WAVE AMR is enabled later.

The midpoint rule is exact for a WAVE step lying within one linearly
interpolated AVAC-frame interval. The implementation does not split a WAVE
step at a frame-time knot, so a step spanning a change in the interpolated
slope has an additional quadrature error. The AVAC output interval and its
alignment with WAVE time steps are therefore coupling-resolution parameters,
not merely visualization choices. The coupled
verification in Sect.~\ref{sec:coupling-verification} examines both temporal
sampling and spatial shoreline resolution.

\subsubsection{Transfer ledger and provenance}

Each prepared WAVE scenario retains the source AVAC run and frame times, all
shoreline faces, the faces that are active in at least one frame, the receiving
source cells, and the count of samples replaced because they were outside
AVAC coverage. In addition, AVAC4QGIS integrates the sum of all stored source
rates by the trapezoidal rule over the AVAC frame times. The resulting ledger
contains injected water volume, both depth-momentum integrals, and both
physical water-momentum components after multiplication by $\rho_w$.

The ledger separates a transfer error from a propagation error. Its volume and
momentum can be compared directly with the positive avalanche flux implied by
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:normal-flux}--\eqref{eq:coupling-beta}, while the WAVE state can
be integrated independently after injection. These two balances form the
first part of the coupling verification in
Sect.~\ref{sec:coupling-verification}.

\subsubsection{Physical scope of the sequential coupling}

The transfer is one-way and offline. AVAC is completed before WAVE starts, so
water pressure and wave motion do not feed back on avalanche runout. The AVAC
state is not removed or altered after crossing the polygon; its inward flux is
used only to construct an equivalent water source. Consequently, the method
does not track submerged granular material or explicitly solve buoyancy,
hydrodynamic drag, entrainment, mixing, compaction, melting, or air
displacement.

Within WAVE, the converted material is inserted into the polygon-mask
shoreline source-target cells (which can include the zero-depth stabilization
rim) and thereafter behaves as water governed by
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:wave-mass}--\eqref{eq:wave-ymomentum}. This construction is less
complete than two-phase or multilayer impact models, but is computationally
transparent and compatible with a geospatial hazard workflow. Its scientific
claim is correspondingly narrow: it is a conservative depth-integrated
shoreline forcing when $\varepsilon_T=1$, followed by hydrostatic long-wave
propagation. The forthcoming laboratory comparison must determine the useful
range of $\varepsilon_T$ and the conditions under which this reduction is
adequate.

\subsection{QGIS integration, managed runtimes, and outputs}
\label{sec:qgis-integration}

AVAC4QGIS embeds the model chain in QGIS rather than requiring the user to
construct GeoClaw data files manually. The plugin reads a selected DEM band,
transforms release and lake polygons into its coordinate reference system,
and provides previews of the release depth, initial snow surface, lake depth,
and stabilized shoreline before execution. Scientific controls remain
explicit: the interface writes the selected rheology, release corrections,
grid spacing, duration, output frequency, wet--dry tolerance, friction, CFL,
and coupling coefficient into human-readable run configuration files.

Preparation checks a valid coordinate reference system and positive square
horizontal cell spacing. Release and lake polygons are transformed into the
selected DEM coordinate reference system. Users must ensure that this CRS has
suitable metric horizontal units, that raster orientation and sampling are
appropriate for a Cartesian calculation, and that DEM and water-level vertical
datums are compatible; these properties cannot in general be inferred from
the layer metadata. The WAVE grid must cover the AVAC domain with an integer
number of cells, and its terrain/bathymetry DEM must cover that domain at an
equal or integer-finer source resolution. A coupled run cannot be prepared from an
unfinished AVAC calculation or from a lake shoreline entirely outside AVAC
coverage. Each calculation is materialized in a new run directory containing
copies of its geospatial inputs, configuration, generated solver files,
status metadata, and outputs. Thus later changes to a QGIS layer do not
silently rewrite the provenance of an existing result.

The numerical executables and their required native libraries are distributed
as managed, platform-specific runtimes. At installation the plugin selects the
runtime for the operating-system/architecture pair, checks its manifest and
cryptographic hashes, and installs it atomically in the QGIS profile. A user
therefore does not need a Fortran compiler, Make, a Python Clawpack
installation, or a separately configured Conda environment. The packaging
system contains target selection for macOS on Apple Silicon, Windows on AMD64,
and Linux on x86\_64; the set of binaries formally released and tested for the
paper is reported separately in the reproducibility matrix rather than
inferred from this selection logic.

After a run, the plugin reads GeoClaw patch output into a shared QGIS results
model. Standard temporal AVAC layers are depth, terrain-tangent speed,
snow-surface elevation, and the derived kinetic-pressure proxy
\begin{equation}
  p_k=\tfrac12\rho_a U_{a,s}^2/1000\quad\text{(kPa)}.
  \label{eq:qgis-kinetic-pressure}
\end{equation}
It is neither a solver pressure nor a basal-stress, impact-pressure, or
constitutive prediction. The raw output frames retain the conserved momenta,
but the ordinary QGIS temporal layer choices do not expose a momentum map.
Summary products include maximum depth, speed, and kinetic pressure, plus
arrival and time-of-maximum fields where available; the last saved temporal
frame supplies the final state rather than a separate deposition product. The
temporal reader currently masks speed and kinetic pressure below a fixed
depth of \SI{0.05}{\metre} and uses an abrupt momentum/depth ratio. It can
therefore disagree with a user-selected $h_v$ or the smooth fixed-grid maximum
diagnostic. WAVE results add lake-volume and shoreline-source diagnostics,
while coupled scenarios retain a link to the source AVAC run and its transfer
ledger. AVAC and WAVE use the same civil-time origin in QGIS, so their layers
can be compared on one timeline even though the solvers execute sequentially.

These controls make the workflow ``plug and play'' in the operational sense:
installation and routine execution do not require solver compilation or
command-line preprocessing. They do not remove the need for scientific
judgement. Release geometry, DEM quality, rheological parameters, water level,
resolution, boundary distance, and the coupling efficiency remain modelling
choices whose consequences must be tested and documented.

\subsection{AVAC verification}
\label{sec:avac-verification}

\paragraph{Checkpoint of the illustrated verification.}
The two Coulomb panels and the water-limit appendix retain the supplied
pre-1.0.0 calculations, identified in their companion JSON by AVAC executable
SHA-256 beginning \texttt{6e85bee646c3}. The values below are synchronized
with that JSON rather than mixed with later Windows runs. These illustrations
are not a new full AMR verification of version \modelversion. For the final
Voellmy stopping correction alone, paired native controls preserved uniform
sloping Coulomb and frictionless water fields exactly; horizontal Kerswell AMR
depth differed by at most \SI{1.15e-15}{\metre}, with unchanged reported
analytical metrics. Earlier changes to the static-interface guard did alter
some granular flat states. Thus the narrow stopping-fix regression is not
evidence that every change since the illustrated checkpoint leaves all flat
trajectories unchanged. No new simulations were run for this documentation
and version-number update.

The supplied manuscript illustrates AVAC verification with two finite-volume Coulomb dam-break problems derived
from the exact shallow-layer solution of \citet{Kerswell2005} and with
closed local tests of the curvature-dependent normal-load source. The horizontal
case isolates pressure-driven spreading, basal resistance, wet--dry
propagation, and natural arrest.  The inclined case retains the same solution
structure while adding the downslope gravity source and the reduction of
normal stress with slope.  Taken together, the tests exercise the parts of
the momentum balance that distinguish AVAC from a water-only shallow-flow
solver.  A third, frictionless water-limit test is reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:avac-verification}.

Each nominally one-dimensional problem was solved by the two-dimensional AVAC
executable in a five-cell-wide strip with wall boundaries in the transverse
direction.  Three AMR levels were used, with two 4:1 longitudinal refinements
and no transverse refinement.  The horizontal case therefore progressed from
a \SI{40}{\milli\metre} background mesh to \SI{2.5}{\milli\metre} near the
front and rear boundaries; the corresponding spacings for the inclined case
were \SI{30}{\milli\metre} and \SI{1.875}{\milli\metre}.  Narrow,
overlapping space--time refinement corridors followed the independently
evaluated analytical boundary paths.  These paths controlled mesh allocation
only: neither the conserved state nor the numerical flux received analytical
information.  Thus the fine mesh remained concentrated near both moving
boundaries while the rest of the domain stayed coarse.

For the horizontal case, the numerical front was obtained from the rightmost
cell whose depth exceeded the solver dry tolerance.  For the inclined case,
the energy-resolved support archived by the validation notebook was used so
that the reported front represents dynamically resolved material. Its
post-processing threshold is
$\sqrt{h_a}|U_h|>\num{5e-5}\,\mathrm{m}^{3/2}\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$; it is
independent of the solver configuration and is not a velocity ceiling. The rear
boundary was the downstream
edge of material whose depth remained within \SI{0.1}{\percent} of $H_0$; this
finite tolerance prevents harmless AMR interpolation perturbations from being
classified as motion.  Boundary errors are
reported as root-mean-square errors (RMSEs) over the corresponding analytical
moving interval.  Depth and velocity were sampled along the strip centreline,
and conservation was evaluated from the integral of flow depth per unit
transverse width.  The notebook for each test records the complete controls,
the solver checksum, the achieved AMR level, centreline fields, and boundary
time series; consequently, Fig.~\ref{fig:avac-coulomb-verification} can be
identified by the supplied diagnostic summary. The rendered figures and
summaries are retained; regenerating any unavailable full native history
requires rerunning the corresponding source and controls.

\subsubsection{Kerswell horizontal Coulomb dam break}

The initial condition was a static column of depth $H_0=\SI{1}{\metre}$ on
$-\SI{10}{\metre}\leq x\leq0$, with a dry bed downstream and a Coulomb
coefficient $\mu=0.1$.  The upstream boundary was a wall and the downstream
boundary was extrapolating.  With
\begin{equation}
  X_0=\frac{H_0}{\mu}=\SI{10}{\metre},\qquad
  T_0=\frac{1}{\mu}\sqrt{\frac{H_0}{g}}=\SI{3.19}{\second},\qquad
  \tau=\frac{t}{T_0},
  \label{eq:kerswell-scales}
\end{equation}
the analytical front advances according to
$x_f/X_0=2\tau-\tau^2/2$ until it arrests at $\tau=2$ and
$x_f/X_0=2$; the rear boundary arrests earlier, at
$\tau=1.529654$ and $x_b/X_0=-0.721577$ \citep{Kerswell2005}.

AVAC reproduced both boundary trajectories and the interior depth and
velocity profiles (Fig.~\ref{fig:avac-coulomb-verification}a--c).  During
motion, the front and rear RMSEs were \SI{0.1585}{\metre} and
\SI{0.00311}{\metre}, respectively.  At \SI{10}{\second}, the simulated front
was at \SI{19.831}{\metre}, compared with the analytical
\SI{20}{\metre}, and the maximum remaining speed was
\SI{1.69e-4}{\metre\per\second}. The depth integral was initially
\SI{10}{\metre\squared} per metre of transverse width and varied by
\SI{6.15e-4}{\metre\squared}, or \SI{6.15e-3}{\percent}.  The agreement in
the arrested state is especially relevant because no prescribed stopping time
or fitted velocity threshold was used: arrest follows from the Coulomb and
static-yield terms described in Sect.~\ref{sec:avac-method}.

\subsubsection{Coulomb dam break on a sloping bed}

The second calculation used the same released depth, but placed the column on
a planar bed descending at $\theta=5^\circ$ and increased the Coulomb
coefficient to $\mu=0.2$.  In the downslope coordinate, gravity and basal
resistance combine into an effective friction coefficient
\begin{equation}
  \mu_{\mathrm{eff}}=\mu-\tan\theta=0.1125,
  \label{eq:inclined-effective-friction}
\end{equation}
while the pressure-wave speed is based on $g\cos\theta$.  Consequently, the
horizontal analytical solution can be reused with
$X_0=H_0/\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}=\SI{8.89}{\metre}$ and
$T_0=\sqrt{H_0/(g\cos\theta)}/\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}=\SI{2.84}{\second}$.
This equivalence provides a direct test of the balance between the topographic
source and Coulomb resistance implemented in AVAC, rather than merely a second
wet--dry propagation test.

The resulting dimensionless trajectories and profiles closely followed the
same analytical curves as the horizontal case
(Fig.~\ref{fig:avac-coulomb-verification}d--f).  The moving-front and rear
RMSEs were \SI{0.1232}{\metre} and \SI{0.0342}{\metre}, respectively. At
\SI{6}{\second}, the illustrated run placed the resolved front at \SI{17.6253}{\metre},
compared with the analytical \SI{17.776}{\metre}; the rear
positions were \SI{-6.5209}{\metre} and \SI{-6.4082}{\metre}. The maximum
resolved speed had decreased to \SI{1.14e-3}{\metre\per\second}. Over the
calculation, the \SI{9.99}{\metre\squared} initial depth integral varied by
\SI{9.07e-4}{\metre\squared}, corresponding to
\SI{9.08e-3}{\percent}. These are the supplied historical diagnostic
definitions, not a recomputation using the current release's controls.

The two Coulomb tests are complementary.  The horizontal calculation shows
that AVAC propagates and arrests a finite granular mass without gravitational
driving along the bed, whereas the inclined calculation shows that the same
behavior is recovered after the driving and resisting accelerations are
combined.  Their common dimensionless solution therefore links the observed
agreement to the implementation of the AVAC source terms, rather than to a
case-specific parameter adjustment.

\subsubsection{Local curvature normal-load source}
\label{sec:avac-curvature-source-verification}

The curvature-dependent normal-load term was checked independently of the
finite-volume flux by compiling the same Fortran rheology module used by AVAC
and comparing its scalar frozen-coefficient update with closed solutions. A
material element with initial map speed \SI{8}{\metre\per\second} and
$\mu=0.30$ was evaluated for \SI{4}{\second} at the nadir of planar,
concave-circular, and convex-circular tracks. The circular radius was
\SI{40}{\metre}, giving $\kappa_v=\pm\SI{0.025}{\per\metre}$, and the
analytical equation was
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\mathrm dU_h}{\mathrm dt}
  =-\mu\left(g+\kappa_vU_h^2\right).
  \label{eq:avac-curvature-verification}
\end{equation}
The maximum absolute speed errors were below
\SI{3.2e-15}{\metre\per\second}. Repeating a \SI{2}{\second} update as
eight equal source substeps changed the result only at machine precision.

A zero-force test of the isolated \emph{scalar basal routine} leaves its input
map speed unchanged. It must not be interpreted as the behaviour of the full
current source step, which first performs the separate terrain-tangent
transport. The production regression tests additionally exercise affine-bed
identity, tangent-speed preservation, the differential connection limit,
rotated two-dimensional geometry, physical boundaries and patch partitioning.
These checks test distinct operators rather than replace a resolved curved-DEM
trajectory or establish convergence of their split composition.

The circular-track values above verify the normal-load term for frozen local
geometry. Their convex state remains below the loss-of-contact threshold in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:avac-contact-speed}; that particular example does not exercise
the branch switch. Compiled source-stopping tests cover the corrected Voellmy
and cohesive-Voellmy zero-result handling, including near-dry states and
unchanged Coulomb/water controls. The released test sources retain these
separate scopes explicitly.



\subsection{WAVE verification}
\label{sec:wave-verification}

The WAVE figures and their numerical summaries below are retained from the
supplied manuscript. They were not rerun for version \modelversion; the
Windows WAVE executable is unchanged from the preceding release. They remain
component-verification evidence at their recorded configurations, not a new
cross-platform equivalence experiment.

\subsubsection{Lake at rest}

The first WAVE test asks whether the lake initialization and the discretized
bathymetric source remain balanced before any avalanche source is applied.
The bed contains the smooth bump used by the Baines steady-flow test, while
the initial free surface is horizontal at \SI{2}{\metre}.  The
\SI{25}{\metre} by \SI{1}{\metre} domain is resolved with
\SI{0.05}{\metre} cells, the longitudinal boundaries are closed, and the
transverse boundaries are periodic.  The coupling file contains no active
source.  Consequently, the exact solution is a lake at rest for the complete
\SI{10}{\second} calculation.

The free-surface errors rose during the initial numerical adjustment and then
remained nearly constant (Fig.~\ref{fig:wave-analytical-verification}a).  The
largest $L_\infty$ and $L_2$ errors over all saved times were
\SI{6.37e-4}{\metre} and \SI{6.11e-5}{\metre}, respectively.  Thus the
topographic balance introduces a submillimetric disturbance, but no growing
wave.  This test is especially relevant to the coupled workflow: it places an
upper bound on the background signal against which an avalanche-generated
impulse must be distinguished.

\subsubsection{Thacker planar oscillation in a paraboloid}

The still-water calculation does not exercise shoreline motion.  We therefore
use the exact planar free-surface oscillation in a parabolic basin derived by
\citet{Thacker1981}, with reference values generated independently through
the SWASHES collection \citep{DelestreEtAl2013}.  A \SI{4}{\metre} square
basin is represented by a $100\times100$ grid, giving
\SI{0.04}{\metre} cells, and wall conditions are imposed on all four sides.
The calculation covers three complete oscillation periods, so that phase,
amplitude, and repeated wetting and drying all contribute to the final error.

Figure~\ref{fig:wave-analytical-verification}b compares the WAVE and
analytical centreline free surfaces over the parabolic topography after the
third period.  The WAVE centreline depth follows the analytical wet support
and profile closely;
the depth RMSE is \SI{1.27e-3}{\metre}, and the maximum absolute error is
\SI{2.84e-3}{\metre}.  Together with the lake-at-rest result, this shows that
WAVE both maintains a nominally stationary lake and transports a moving
shoreline through a genuinely two-dimensional basin without a systematic
phase shift visible at the reported resolution.

\subsection{Coupling verification}
\label{sec:coupling-verification}

The component tests above verify the two solvers separately, but not the
transfer between them.  We therefore prescribed a spatially uniform synthetic
AVAC state along a straight shoreline of length $L=\SI{0.8}{\metre}$.  Over
$T=\SI{1}{\second}$, its depth was $h_a=\SI{0.1}{\metre}$ and its velocity
was
\begin{equation}
  (u_a,v_a)=A(t)(0.02,0.01)\;\si{\metre\per\second}, \qquad
  A(t)=\sin(\pi t/T)\left(1+\frac{t}{4T}\right).
  \label{eq:coupling-verification-pulse}
\end{equation}
The pulse is zero at both ends and has closed-form volume and momentum
integrals.  It was written as native AVAC frames, sampled by the production
shoreline converter, serialized in the conservative source format, read by
the WAVE Fortran source module, and applied by the current WAVE executable.
Thus the test exercises the complete numerical transfer rather than only the
Python rate calculation.

For a uniform active shoreline, the expected transferred volume,
depth-integrated momentum, and physical water momentum are
\begin{align}
  V_w &= \beta L\int_0^T h_a u_n\,\mathrm{d}t, \\
  I_x &= \beta L\int_0^T h_a u_n u_a\,\mathrm{d}t, \qquad P_x=\rho_w I_x, \\
  I_y &= \beta L\int_0^T h_a u_n v_a\,\mathrm{d}t, \qquad P_y=\rho_w I_y,
  \label{eq:coupling-verification-ledger}
\end{align}
where $u_n$ is the velocity component directed into the lake and $\beta=1$
for this verification.  Equation~\eqref{eq:coupling-verification-pulse} gives
$V_w=\SI{1.14592e-3}{\metre\cubed}$,
$I_x=\SI{2.02827e-5}{\metre\tothe{4}\per\second}$, and
$I_y=\SI{1.01413e-5}{\metre\tothe{4}\per\second}$.  The WAVE domain was a
frictionless, periodic \SI{2}{\metre} by \SI{1}{\metre} basin with an
initial uniform depth of \SI{0.5}{\metre}.  Periodicity removes boundary
losses, so changes in the three globally integrated conserved variables can
be compared directly with the written source ledger.

The prescribed and serialized volume-rate histories are visually coincident
(Fig.~\ref{fig:coupling-verification}a).  WAVE was run on uniform
\SI{0.10}{\metre} and \SI{0.05}{\metre} grids and with two AMR levels from a
\SI{0.10}{\metre} base grid to a \SI{0.01}{\metre} finest grid.  Integration
of the non-overlapping native control volumes gave a maximum absolute relative
closure error of \num{5.61e-5} across volume and both momentum components
(Fig.~\ref{fig:coupling-verification}b).  The AMR run reached level 2 and
reduced the largest closure error to \num{2.95e-7}; the result therefore
confirms that the format-2 total rates remain conservative when the receiving
cell area changes.

\section{ISeeSnow avalanche-model intercomparison}
\label{sec:iseesnow}

\subsection{Protocol and common metrics}

The ISeeSnow v1.0 exercise provides common idealized and real topographies,
release areas, rheological parameters, and submitted peak fields from several
depth-integrated avalanche models \citep{WirbelEtAl2026}.  We ran AVAC on the
supplied \SI{5}{\metre} grid without retuning the prescribed physical
parameters. Numerical limiter and shallow-flow controls were assessed using
peer PFT comparisons, as distinguished below. The three calculations are the
idealized Voellmy case ($\mu=0.4$, $\xi=\SI{2000}{\metre\per\second\squared}$),
the real-topography Voellmy case ($\mu=0.2$ with the same $\xi$), and the
idealized Coulomb-only case ($\mu=0.4$ and no turbulent resistance).  In all
three cases, the prescribed terrain-normal release thickness is
\SI{1.5}{\metre}.

The two idealized cases share the same inclined plane, variable-width ravine,
release area, and flat foreland, while the real-terrain case uses a distinct
mountain catchment and release polygon (Fig.~\ref{fig:iseesnow-cases}).  Thus
the first and third calculations contrast Voellmy and Coulomb-only resistance
on a common geometry, using the model-specific numerical controls stated
below, whereas the first and
second retain Voellmy resistance while changing both terrain and the
prescribed Coulomb coefficient.

Because ISeeSnow prescribes release thickness normal to the terrain whereas
AVAC advances vertical map-cell depth, initialization applies the inverse
geometric conversion
\begin{equation}
  h_a(x,y,0)=\frac{h_n(x,y,0)}{\cos\phi(x,y)}.
  \label{eq:iseesnow-initial-depth}
\end{equation}
The prescribed normal release volume is therefore represented consistently
on sloping cells before the calculation begins.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{iseesnow_case_setup.pdf}
  \caption{The three AVAC ISeeSnow configurations, drawn from the supplied
  benchmark DEMs and release polygons.  (a) Idealized topography with Voellmy
  resistance, (b) real topography with Voellmy resistance, and (c) the same
  idealized topography with Coulomb resistance only.  Red polygons denote the
  common \SI{1.5}{\metre} terrain-normal release thickness.  The maps are an

  \label{fig:iseesnow-cases}
\end{figure}

AVAC stores vertical depth and horizontal map-coordinate momentum, whereas
ISeeSnow prescribes terrain-normal peak flow thickness (PFT). We therefore
convert vertical peak depth back to terrain-normal PFT cell by cell as
$\mathrm{PFT}_n=\mathrm{PFT}_h\cos\phi$. PFV is the physical terrain-tangent
magnitude consistent with Eq.~\eqref{eq:avac-geometry},
$[u_a^2+v_a^2+(u_a\partial_xB+v_a\partial_yB)^2]^{1/2}$, evaluated at every
solver step before taking the temporal maximum. The release state at $t=0$ is
included explicitly in the PFT maximum, and subsequent maxima are obtained
from the native peak-output calculation. PFV is zero for vertical depth at or
below \SI{0.05}{\metre}, uses the smooth diagnostic transition described in
Sect.~\ref{sec:avac-method} between \SI{0.05}{\metre} and
\SI{0.20}{\metre}, and is the exact terrain-tangent velocity at larger depth.
This follows the model-specific minimum-depth criteria documented by
ISeeSnow. The AVAC run configuration sets the shared GeoClaw speed limit to
$10^{99}$~\si{\metre\per\second}, so neither the evolving state nor the
reported PFV is clipped by a finite velocity ceiling.

Two peer populations serve different questions. The scalar overview uses the
published Table C1 core group: 11 idealized-Voellmy, 10 real-terrain, and
11 Coulomb submissions. It compares thalweg runout, maximum PFT and maximum
PFV. Runout is the furthest thalweg coordinate with normal PFT exceeding
\SI{0.5}{\metre}; it is not the area of a \SI{0.01}{\metre} support mask.
The off-scale Coulomb PFV of r.avaflow is retained in all scalar statistics
and annotated in the figure, not discarded.

Spatial PFT comparison, the primary numerical-selection criterion, instead
uses only rasters with exactly matching dimensions, cell size and sample
coordinates: 9, 8 and 8 peers for the three cases. No field is shifted or
resampled to improve agreement. Its dimensionless score is
\begin{equation}
 E_{\mathrm{PFT}} =
 \mathop{\mathrm{median}}_i
 \left[
 \frac{\mathrm{RMSE}(\mathrm{PFT}_{\mathrm{AVAC}},\mathrm{PFT}_i)}
 {\mathop{\mathrm{median}}_{j\ne i}
  \mathrm{RMSE}(\mathrm{PFT}_i,\mathrm{PFT}_j)}
 \right],
 \label{eq:iseesnow-pft-score}
\end{equation}
using full-grid errors and the recorded common nodata treatment. It is not
the unnormalized median RMSE in metres, nor an observational accuracy score.
The broader scalar population must not be confused with this spatially
aligned population.

The current comparison uses the accepted executable whose SHA-256 begins
\texttt{55a8c4baf0cc}; version \modelversion\ contains the same binary.
All cases use a uniform \SI{5}{\metre} grid, second-order spatial updating,
a \SI{1200}{\second} horizon and \SI{10}{\second} saved-state intervals.
The two Voellmy cases use Van Leer and target CFL 0.50; Coulomb uses Minmod
and target CFL 0.25. The correction-flux depth controls are respectively
\SI{0.10}{\metre} and \SI{0.05}{\metre}; the solver dry tolerance is
\SI{1e-4}{\metre} and the rejection ceiling is CFL 1.0. These are explicit
benchmark controls, not an assertion that Van Leer or CFL 0.25 is the general
plugin default. Physical friction coefficients and curvature treatment were
not fitted to peer output, but the numerical choices were assessed using
PFT agreement; this is therefore not a blind validation exercise.

\subsection{Conservation, practical rest and remaining sensitivity}

The native relative volume changes $(V_f-V_0)/V_0$ are
\num{1.6463e-9}, \num{6.0960e-10} and \num{-6.4338e-10} for idealized
Voellmy, real-terrain Voellmy and Coulomb. Practical-rest confirmation occurs
at \SI{590}{\second}, \SI{520}{\second} and \SI{100}{\second}, respectively,
and remains satisfied at subsequent saved checks through the horizon.
This criterion requires less than 1\% of the release volume to be moving
faster than \SI{0.01}{\metre\per\second} in the resolved depth band above
\SI{0.05}{\metre}, for at least three saved times. It is not zero velocity in
every wet cell. Final resolved moving volumes are approximately
\SI{70.09}{\metre\cubed}, \SI{231.35}{\metre\cubed} and zero; motion below the
reporting-depth threshold is audited separately.

The correction that honors Voellmy's exact zero source result removed the
reproduced extremely large near-dry post-source states. Both full Voellmy
observation-only audits reproduced all 484 saved native artifacts and the
PFT/PFV fields; no invalid accepted post-source states were observed.
However, real terrain still has a raw all-step terrain-tangent maximum of
\SI{90.97}{\metre\per\second} in the \SIrange{0.05}{0.10}{\metre} depth band,
distinct from its filtered PFV of \SI{67.66}{\metre\per\second}. Neither saved
frames nor filtered peak fields bound every intermediate split state.

A same-source Voellmy target-CFL refinement from 0.50 to 0.25 reduced the
real-terrain thin-layer pulse and improved its normalized PFT score by about
1.52\%, but worsened the idealized PFT score by about 1.84\%. PFV and local
fields changed non-uniformly. Accordingly, the existing controls are retained;
two timestep levels do not establish a convergence rate or universal
stability. The improved PFT comparisons, the separate flat-control evidence
and these remaining sensitivities are reported together rather than treating
peer agreement as a substitute for numerical verification.

\section{Additional AVAC verification}
\label{app:avac-verification}

As an end-member test, AVAC was also configured with zero basal friction and
no avalanche rheology and compared with the exact finite-volume dam-break
solution of \citet{AnceyEtAl2008}.  A triangular prism of water with
$H_0=\SI{1}{\metre}$ was released on a frictionless $10^\circ$ planar bed;
its initial depth was
$h(x,0)=H_0(1-x/x_b)$ for $x_b\leq x\leq0$, where
$x_b=-H_0/\tan(10^\circ)$.  The calculation used the same three-level,
front-focused AMR procedure as the main AVAC tests on a domain extending from
$x=-\SI{10}{\metre}$ to $x=\SI{60}{\metre}$, progressing from a
\SI{40}{\milli\metre} background spacing to
\SI{2.5}{\milli\metre} near the head and tail.  This case is placed in the
appendix because it verifies the frictionless shallow-water limit shared with
GeoClaw, rather than avalanche-specific resistance.

A constant \SI{10}{\metre} vertical datum was retained in the planar-bed
input used for this benchmark. The present AVAC configuration sets GeoClaw's
reference sea level to $-10^4$~m, safely below the computational terrain, so
the translation is no longer required to keep newly refined dry cells dry.
It is nevertheless immaterial to the comparison because a uniform vertical
translation changes neither bed gradients, water depth, nor the analytical
solution.

The extended domain retained both moving boundaries throughout the complete
\SI{5}{\second} comparison.  Over that interval, the front and rear RMSEs
were \SI{0.06266}{\metre} and \SI{0.08056}{\metre}, respectively. The
maximum initial-depth discrepancy on
the uniform diagnostic grid was \SI{3.31}{\milli\metre}.  The initial depth
integral, \SI{2.83564}{\metre\squared} per metre of transverse width, differed
from the exact value by \SI{1.31e-7}{\metre\squared} and varied by
\SI{4.15e-4}{\metre\squared} over the full simulation, or
\SI{0.0146}{\percent}.  These errors include the interpolation from the
front-focused AMR hierarchy to the one-row diagnostic grid.
